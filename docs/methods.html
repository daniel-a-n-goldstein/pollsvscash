Polls vs. Cash

the hope index

Every number on the tracker comes from the pipeline below. Nothing is hand-tuned after the fact.

What the wedge is

For each race we compare two probabilities: what current polling implies and what prediction-market prices imply. The wedge is their difference in percentage points:

wedge = market price − poll-implied probability

A positive (amber, “hope”) wedge means people betting real money are more optimistic than the survey data. A negative (teal, “doubt”) wedge means the money is more skeptical. The wedge deliberately conflates two things: information the polls haven't absorbed yet, and sentiment — the hopes, fears, and biases of the betting crowd. This page can't tell you which is which on any given day. Watching which one it turns out to be is the point of the tracker.

Market prices

Prices come from Polymarket's public API, using the liquid party-level contract for each race (e.g., “Will the Democrats win the Maine Senate race in 2026?”). A price of 61.5¢ for a $1 payout is read as a 61.5% implied probability. Prices are last-trade prices, fetched once daily at ~06:30 UTC by an automated script.

Poll benchmarks

The poll benchmark for each race is the RealClearPolling average, pulled automatically once a week (Sundays) from RCP's public data feed. I don't pick the polls or do any averaging myself; the one choice is RCP over the other aggregators, mostly because it has a page for every race on the board and its average is the one most people already quote. The source and date range of each benchmark are shown under the race on the tracker. The House row uses RCP's generic ballot average, which is a rough proxy for House control, not a model of it. A polling margin is converted to a win probability with a normal (probit) model of historical polling error:

P(win) = Φ(margin / σ),  σ = 5.5 points

σ = 5.5 reflects the typical error of state-level polling averages several months before an election. So a candidate up 5.5 points gets ~84%, and a tied race gets 50%. As the election nears, true σ shrinks and this conversion becomes conservative.

Senate control has no polling margin to convert, so its benchmark is a published model probability rather than a poll: currently Decision Desk HQ's Senate forecast, entered by hand about once a week and dated in the data file. That row is the one place on the board where the "polls" side is someone else's model rather than a polling average, so read its wedge accordingly.

How to read the wedge (the assumptions that matter)

Assumption / artifactWhat it does to the wedge
Favorite–longshot biasMarkets rarely price above ~90¢ or below ~10¢ even for near-certainties, because parking cash until November for a penny of upside isn't worth it. Big wedges on lopsided races (e.g., polls at 97%, market at 87%) are mostly this — mechanical, not sentiment.
Thin marketsRaces flagged “thin market” trade on little money; one trader can move them. Their wedges are noisy.
Polls aren't ground truthHouse effects, herding, and differential response mean the poll benchmark has its own error. Some of the wedge is poll error, not market opinion.
Who is bettingPrediction-market traders are not a representative sample of anyone. The wedge measures the mood of a particular moneyed, extremely online crowd — that's the object of interest here, not a bug.
σ = 5.5 is a choiceA bigger σ pulls poll probabilities toward 50 and shrinks wedges on lopsided races; a smaller σ does the reverse. The qualitative pattern is robust; exact wedge sizes are not.
Pre-launch historyMarket prices before launch (October 9, 2026) are backfilled from Polymarket's daily price history, so that part of the chart is real. The poll line is not: I don't have RCP's averages for those dates, so it is held flat at the launch value and drawn dashed. Wedges before launch should be ignored. From launch onward, every point is a daily snapshot.

Update cadence

A scheduled GitHub Action fetches market prices daily at ~06:30 UTC, recomputes the wedge, and republishes the data — no manual step. A second script refreshes the RCP poll benchmarks every Sunday; polling averages barely move day to day, and a weekly step keeps the two lines visibly different kinds of information. If a fetch fails, the previous value is carried forward and logged. When a race resolves, it is kept in the data with its outcome, so over time the tracker accumulates a public track record of whether hope or doubt was right.

This is a data project about measuring public mood, not investment or voting advice.
