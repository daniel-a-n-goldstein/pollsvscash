The Hope Index

polls vs. cash

Every number on the tracker comes from the pipeline below. Nothing is hand-tuned after the fact.

What the hope wedge is

For each race, two probabilities are compared: what current polling implies and what prediction-market prices imply. The wedge is their difference in percentage points:

wedge = market price − poll-implied probability

A positive (amber, “hope”) wedge means people betting real money are more optimistic than the polling data. A negative (teal, “doubt”) wedge means the money is more skeptical. The wedge conflates two things: information the polls haven't absorbed yet, and sentiment — the hopes, fears, and biases of the betting crowd. The wedge can't tell the difference. Seeing which one it turns out to be is what the tracker is for.

Market prices

Prices come from Polymarket's public API, using the liquid party-level contract for each race (e.g., “Will the Democrats win the Maine Senate race in 2026?”). For example, a price of 61.5¢ for a $1 payout is read as a 61.5% implied probability. Prices are last-trade prices, fetched once daily at ~06:30 UTC by an automated script.

Poll benchmarks

The poll benchmark for each race is the RealClearPolling average, pulled automatically once a week on Sundays from RCP's public data feed. I don't pick the polls or do any averaging myself. RCP was used because it has a page for every race on the board. The source and date range of each benchmark are shown under the race on the tracker. The House row uses RCP's generic ballot average, which is a rough proxy for House control, not a model of it. A polling margin is converted to a win probability with a normal (probit) model of historical polling error:

P(win) = Φ(margin / σ),  σ = 5.5 points

σ = 5.5 is roughly the historical error of state-level polling averages against the result, and it is held fixed through election day so the wedge series stays comparable over time rather than shifting with the knob. For example, a candidate up 5.5 points gets ~84%, and a tied race gets 50%. If anything it is slightly conservative for the final weeks, which pulls poll probabilities toward 50 and makes wedges on lopsided races look a little larger.

Senate control has no polling margin to convert, so its benchmark is a published model probability rather than a poll: currently Decision Desk HQ's Senate forecast, entered by hand about once a week and dated in the data file. This row is the only one where the "polls" side is a model rather than a polling average, so it should be read accordingly.

How to read the hope wedge (the assumptions that matter)

Assumption / artifactWhat it does to the wedge
Favorite–longshot biasMarkets rarely price above ~90¢ or below ~10¢ even for near-certainties, because parking cash until November for a penny of upside isn't worth it. Big wedges on lopsided races (e.g., polls at 97%, market at 87%) are mostly this mechanical outcome rather than sentiment.
Thin marketsSome of these markets trade on little money (a few hundred thousand dollars over a year), so one trader can move the price and their wedges are noisy.
Polls aren't "ground truth"House effects, herding, and differential response mean the poll benchmark has its own error. Some of the wedge is poll error, not market opinion.
Who is bettingPrediction-market traders are not a representative sample of anyone. The wedge measures the mood of a particular, probably extremely online, crowd. But perhaps that crowd reads sentiment faster than the polls do?
σ = 5.5 is a choiceA bigger σ pulls poll probabilities toward 50 and shrinks wedges on lopsided races. A smaller σ does the reverse. Hence, the qualitative pattern is more robust than exact wedge sizes.
Pre-launch historyMarket prices before launch (October 9, 2026) are backfilled from Polymarket's daily price history back to August 15. The poll line before launch is an estimate, drawn dashed: RCP doesn't publish its past averages, so for each pre-launch day I take the simple mean margin of the RCP-listed polls whose field period ended in the previous 28 days, widened to the most recent three polls whenever fewer than three fall in the window, and convert it with the same probit. It tracks RCP's current average to within about a point, but it is not the same thing. This means that pre-launch wedges should be read loosely. From launch onward, every point is a daily snapshot with RCP's own number.

Update cadence

A scheduled GitHub Action fetches market prices daily at ~06:30 UTC, recomputes the wedge, and republishes the data. A second script refreshes the RCP poll benchmarks every Sunday. Polling averages barely move day to day, and a weekly step keeps the two lines visibly different kinds of information. If a fetch fails, the previous value is carried forward and logged. When a race resolves, it is kept in the data with its outcome, so over time the tracker accumulates a public track record of whether hope or doubt was right.

NOTE: this is a data project by Daniel A. N. Goldstein about measuring public mood, not investment or voting advice. Code & data on GitHub.
